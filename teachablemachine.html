Our Project Statement

Our machine is simple: throw up two fingers, and it detects a peace sign. Make a fist, lift your index and pinky fingers, and it will read a “rock” sign. Curl your fingers in any manner that resembles half a heart, and that is precisely what it will interpret. Finally, make a fist and lift your thumb; this gesture will be interpreted by the machine as a thumbs up. These are some of the most universal hand gestures of the Western world, but with no context, or humanity, a machine must form a schema using only the visual data fed into it. Machines and computers are both a code conglomerate and a fresh young mind waiting to be shaped. In other words, despite having a complex base, Google’s Teachable Machines independently know nothing until you train them.

Poetic irony aside, the process of training a machine requires many skills we did not anticipate. Despite having many images, the machine still required careful attention to detail regarding the quality of the images it was trained on. This model required images that were taken directly on the website using the sample feature that the machine recognized. Though tedious, the photos taken, even if similar, were not learnable. Furthermore, the machine pulls from the data through the most literal of means. Without some level of variation, either through multiple backgrounds, angles, or another presentation, the machine may only detect symbols under extremely specific circumstances. For example, if I had a gray background with good lighting, only showing the peace sign with my palm facing the camera, anyone testing my machine would need to replicate that exact set up.

On a larger scale, this mirrors the core struggle in ⁣Unmasking AI that Joy Buolamwini found when testing the specific facial recognition software that inspired her to speak out about bias in the tech space. The software was trained on poor visual data. It had plenty of quality Caucasian photos to train from, but the developers overlooked other communities. Machines are only as good as the humans that teach it. With the advancements of technology and AI, it is easy to forget that humans are at the center of all of it.

Moreover, learning is doing. In a world where machines do many tasks faster and more efficiently than people, it’s easy to ignore how it works at all. It is even easier to trust that this machine will pick up on all human subtleties and innate assumptions. However, without real experimentation, not only will it make mistakes, but it won't understand why. Joy Buolamwini faced a massive obstacle when she encountered software that was simply unable to read her face unless she wore a featureless white mask. As an experienced programmer herself, Joy knew that there was something more malignant under the surface. Her work exposed a larger issue of what centuries of underrepresentation have done to the current tech industry.

Joy did not believe all programmers intentionally left black women or any other minority behind. The issue was a combination of existing systemic prejudice, reflected in training data, and de-prioritizing inclusion in development. It all ties back to the overarching ideas of this class. A history of exclusion leads to a history where only certain stories are told. When only certain stories are told, they are the most accessible to use as data. When a team of developers consists only of those who fit these stories, they may not even think of those left behind. If a team is told to prioritize profit and efficiency, inclusion feels like a waste of time.

That being said, the world of technology is not doomed. Technology is rapidly evolving and has become more accessible than ever before. Deterministic rhetoric is routinely challenged by Buolamwini's work and the work of many others. On a small scale, this project taught us a simple but valuable lesson that everything can be revised. Despite struggling with navigating this new technology, we never struggled with anything beyond minor inconveniences to get our machine in working order. “We do not have to accept that if AI tools have been adopted, we cannot reverse course. We do not have to accept that if companies have already created a product, it is a foregone conclusion that the product will be used.” (Buolamwini 276). The people working on industrial, massive machine programs face a far greater struggle, but the tech world cannot continue to cut corners.

Technology should adapt to people. This project helped us understand that developing and teaching machines involves more than improving the technology and making sure it works; it involves the people interacting with it. Although our machine was trained to recognize simple hand gestures, such as the heart symbol, peace sign, rock symbol, and thumbs-up, human behavior and decisions are still involved. Our machine recognizes these symbols, but it also may not consider an individual’s ability to make these gestures or the way in which they perform them. It serves as a surface-level take on Buolamwini’s work because it raises the question of whether technology is designed to accommodate human differences or whether people are required to fit into the categories the machine recognizes.

It’s important to consistently question technology. We must be careful about trusting machines and technology 100% of the time. Even though our machine correctly interprets and identifies the four hand gestures, we had a few minor hiccups with teaching the machine using the original data that we collected. This demonstrated that even when technology appears to work correctly, there can be underlying limitations that are not immediately noticeable. When Buolamwini's face was not detected by the facial recognition software she was using, she questioned why the technology failed. Instead of simply accepting the software’s results, she discovered that there were larger issues surrounding racial bias within the software’s design. Our experience taught us the significance of both questioning technological results and understanding that machines are not always reliable.

Ultimately, technology does not exist as a monolith. It is somewhat of an “imagine a billion dollars” umbrella term. Most people could not define it or conceptualize it in their minds, but they know it when they see it. To different degrees, we all interact with technology on a daily basis. These machines, like humans, make mistakes, but the repair and prevention of these mistakes rely on human intervention. Artificial intelligence is far from self-sustaining, and Unmasking AI provides a gripping case study on this very concept. Through experimentation, refining data, programming with an adaptive mindset, and questioning results, developers can progress laterally and produce technology that reflects cultural progression.
